Case 01

Knowing when not to automate

Clinical review workflow, neurosurgical planning
Problem framingAI judgmentClinical trustValidation design
QThe problem

Specialist reviewers were spending a large share of every review working out which of roughly twenty imaging outputs mattered for a given patient’s lesion. The ask was simple: rank them automatically.

+What I found

“Relevant” was really two different questions. Which outputs sit near the lesion is a geometry problem, and it can be computed. Which ones matter for this surgery is clinical judgment, and it changes from surgeon to surgeon. A proximity ranking would also have surfaced the least trustworthy signal first, since known false positives cluster closest to the lesion, while pushing down structures that had shifted from their usual position, often the ones that matter most.

★How I approached it

Rather than automate the judgment, I scoped a deterministic spatial aid that shows clinicians the geometry clearly and leaves the relevance decision with them. The human stays in charge by design, not by disclaimer, and clinicians get the transparency they need to trust what they’re looking at. Before it reaches a live workflow, it runs silently against already-reviewed cases and is checked against what reviewers actually marked.

?Still open

Whether spatial lookup is really where review time goes, which still needs measuring; whether labels like “review first” could anchor less experienced reviewers; and whether the aid changes the product’s regulatory scope.

Case 02

Making release governance legible

Platform configuration across sites, markets and modules
Systems thinkingData modellingGovernance
QThe problem

One platform, many realities: different markets, regulatory states, modules and customer-specific configurations. Nobody could say with confidence exactly what each site was running, and single hospitals increasingly needed commercial, research and study work running side by side.

+What I found

Three internal source documents described three incompatible versioning models: one tied a version to the package a customer buys, another to their subscription. Commercial packaging and software versioning were being treated as the same thing, and runtime feature flags were doing the job that frozen, approved releases should do.

★How I approached it

Separate what’s sold from what runs. Packages describe entitlements; versions describe a pinned, reproducible set of software. One test decides whether a change needs a new version: does it alter what a clinician sees as a finding? I proposed an engagement layer between customer and version, so a single hospital can run several streams, each pinned per protocol, with routing that is deterministic at intake and fails closed whenever anything is ambiguous.

?Still open

Whether an urgent safety fix may override a contractual version pin, a policy call that sits with regulatory leadership. A per-run processing record also still needs to exist, so any result can be traced back to exactly what produced it.

How I work

Structured, collaborative, outcome-oriented.

I bring clarity to complexity without losing sight of the people using the product or the teams building it.

01

Start with the problem

Understand the user, the workflow, the constraints and the evidence before jumping to a solution.

Separate symptoms from root causes

Make assumptions explicit

Look for the person who predicts adoption, often the operator rather than the champion

02

Make trade-offs visible

Use shared criteria to navigate competing needs, limited capacity, risk and uncertainty.

Clarify impact and urgency

Keep what was ordered, authorised and delivered as separate records

Document decisions and the reasoning behind them

03

Create execution clarity

Give teams enough context to make good decisions while keeping scope and outcomes clear.

Define requirements, edge cases and failure points

Give every open decision a named owner

Agree on acceptance and release criteria up front

04

Close the learning loop

Treat launch as the start of learning, not the end of the process.

Instrument before automating: capture why work gets redone

Learn from support cases and incidents

Feed evidence back into the roadmap

My guiding principle

Build for the real problem, not just the loudest request. Good product decisions account for user value, business viability, technical feasibility, and the operational reality it takes to deliver reliably.
